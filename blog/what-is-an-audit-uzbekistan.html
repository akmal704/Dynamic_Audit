What Is an Audit, and Why Does Anyone Need One?

A plain explanation of why companies pay strangers to check their own books — and what that has to do with you.

October 2026
22 min read
Dynamic Audit
Language / Язык / Til:

Start with a problem, not a definition

Suppose your cousin asks you to put 200 million soum into his business. He is a good person. You have known him your whole life. He opens a laptop and shows you a page of numbers that he typed himself. Revenue, costs, profit. The business, the page says, earns 80 million soum a year.

Do you believe it?

Probably you believe that he believes it. That is not the same thing. He might be counting a contract that hasn't been signed. He might be forgetting the money he owes his supplier. He might be including his own salary as profit. He might, if things have gone badly and he is embarrassed, be rounding in a direction that flatters him. None of this requires him to be dishonest. It only requires him to be human, and to be the one holding the pen.

Now notice what happens to you. Because you cannot check the page, you have to assume the worst case you can imagine. You offer him less money, or you demand a bigger share, or you ask for a guarantee on his apartment, or you walk away. Your caution is rational. It is also expensive — for him, and in the end for everyone, because businesses that deserve money don't get it.

This is the problem that auditing exists to solve. Not fraud. Not tax. The problem is that the person who knows the numbers and the person who needs the numbers are two different people, and the second one has no way to check the first.

Everything in this article follows from that one sentence.

The agency problem, explained without the jargon

Economists have a name for this situation, and it is worth learning because once you see it you see it everywhere: the agency problem.

An "agency relationship" exists whenever one person (the principal) hires another person (the agent) to act on their behalf and to make decisions for them. You are the principal when you give your money to a company director to run a business for you. You are the principal when you deposit your salary in a bank and someone else decides who to lend it to. You are the principal when your pension contributions are invested by a fund manager you have never met.

The phrase was given its modern form by Michael Jensen and William Meckling in a 1976 paper in the Journal of Financial Economics, though the underlying observation is older — Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means described the separation of ownership from control in American corporations in 1932, and Adam Smith complained about it in 1776. The structure of the problem is always the same, and it has three parts.

First, the interests differ. The owner wants the business to be worth more. The manager wants a comfortable life, a larger department, a company car, and a bonus this year rather than a better business in five years. These are not evil desires. They are simply different desires, and the manager is the one making the decisions.

Second, the information is unequal. The manager knows what is actually happening in the business. The owner knows what the manager tells them. This asymmetry is not accidental; it is structural. The manager is there every day and the owner is not. That is the entire point of hiring a manager.

Third — and this is the part people miss — the owner knows all of this. The owner is not naïve. The owner knows the manager has different interests and better information. So the owner discounts everything the manager says.

Jensen and Meckling called the total cost of this situation agency costs, and they broke it into three pieces:

  • Monitoring costs — what the principal spends watching the agent. Board meetings, reports, inspections, auditors.
  • Bonding costs — what the agent spends proving they are trustworthy. This is the surprising one, and we will come back to it.
  • Residual loss — the value that is destroyed anyway, because no amount of watching and proving makes the gap disappear completely.

Here is the thing to hold on to: agency costs are real costs. They come out of the value of the business. If you can reduce them, you have created wealth out of nothing but better information. That is what an audit does, and that is why anyone is willing to pay for one.

Where the audit fits

An audit is a monitoring device that the principal and the agent both agree to use, carried out by a third party who works for neither of them.

The auditor's job is not to run the business or to decide whether it is a good business. The auditor's job is narrower and stranger than that: to examine the account of events that management has written, and to state publicly whether that account can be relied upon.

That is all. And it is enough.

Why the manager asks to be audited

Most people assume audit is something done to a company — an imposition, a cost, a regulatory nuisance. Historically, the opposite is closer to the truth.

Return to your cousin. He knows his numbers are honest. He also knows you can't tell the difference between him and a man whose numbers are not honest. So you treat him as an average of the two. He is being punished for other people's dishonesty, and there is nothing he can say that fixes it, because anything he says is also just him talking.

George Akerlof described this mechanism in 1970 in a paper about used cars — "The Market for Lemons," which won him a Nobel Prize. When buyers cannot distinguish good from bad, they pay an average price. Sellers of genuinely good goods refuse to sell at the average price and withdraw. The average quality of what remains falls. Buyers lower their offers again. In the limit, the market collapses, and the people hurt most are the honest sellers.

The only escape is a credible signal — something the honest seller can do that a dishonest one cannot imitate cheaply. A mechanic's inspection, in Akerlof's example. An auditor's report, in ours.

This is what Jensen and Meckling meant by bonding costs: money the agent spends voluntarily, on himself, to buy the principal's trust. And the historical evidence supports it. Ross Watts and Jerold Zimmerman, writing in the Journal of Law and Economics in 1983, showed that English companies hired auditors for centuries before any statute required it. Merchant partnerships, guilds, and early joint-stock ventures appointed people to examine the accounts because investors would not hand over money otherwise. The law, when it eventually arrived, was codifying a practice the market had already invented.

The sequence is worth stating plainly, because it reverses the usual assumption:

Audit was not invented by regulators to control business. It was invented by business to attract capital. Regulation came afterwards, to make it compulsory for the companies most likely to skip it.

So when a growing Uzbek company commissions an audit it does not legally need, it is not being cautious or vain. It is doing exactly what English merchants did in the 1700s: paying money to make its own word worth something.

So what is an audit, precisely?

Now the definition, which will make more sense than it would have four sections ago.

An audit is an independent examination of financial statements, performed to an established professional standard, resulting in a formal opinion as to whether those statements present the entity's financial position and results fairly, in accordance with a stated financial reporting framework.

Five things in that sentence carry all the weight.

"Independent." The auditor must have no interest in the answer. Not a shareholder, not a relative of management, not dependent on this one client for survival, not auditing work they themselves prepared. Independence is not a nice quality that improves an audit. It is the product. An opinion from someone with a stake in the outcome has no information content at all, and would be worth exactly nothing.

"Financial statements." Not the business. Not the strategy. Not whether the director is a decent person. The subject of the audit is a specific set of documents: the statement of financial position, the statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income, the statement of changes in equity, the statement of cash flows, and the notes. Management prepares them. The auditor examines them. The accounts remain management's accounts throughout — this is a point of law and of principle, and it is widely misunderstood.

"To an established standard." In Uzbekistan, independent audits are conducted under the International Standards on Auditing (ISAs) issued by the IAASB, which apply directly. The standards govern everything: how risk is assessed, how much evidence is enough, how samples are chosen, how the file is documented, how the opinion is worded. Without them, "audit" would mean whatever each auditor wanted it to mean, and the signature would carry no information.

"An opinion." Not a certificate. Not a guarantee. Not a statement of fact. An opinion, by a qualified professional, on a defined question — supported by evidence and backed by that professional's licence, liability and reputation.

"Fairly, in accordance with a stated framework." The question is never "are these numbers true?" in some absolute sense. The question is "do these statements comply with IFRS?" or "do they comply with Uzbek national accounting standards?" The framework must be named, because the same underlying business produces different legitimate numbers under different frameworks.

Reasonable assurance, not certainty

ISA 200 requires the auditor to obtain reasonable assurance — a high but not absolute level of confidence. This is not professional cowardice. It is arithmetic.

A mid-sized company generates hundreds of thousands of transactions a year. Examining every one of them would cost more than the company is worth and take longer than the year being audited. So auditors work with materiality (a threshold below which an error would not change any reasonable reader's decision) and sampling (testing a selection and reasoning about the whole).

A reader who understands this understands why an audit opinion is valuable and why it is not a guarantee. The auditor is not saying "every figure is exactly right." The auditor is saying "I have obtained enough appropriate evidence to conclude that these statements are free from material misstatement — that is, free from error large enough to matter."

What an audit is not

The distance between what auditors do and what the public assumes they do has a name in the academic literature: the expectation gap. The term is usually credited to Carl Liggio in 1974; Brenda Porter's 1993 study broke it into a "reasonableness gap" (the public expects things no auditor could deliver) and a "performance gap" (auditors sometimes fall short of what they reasonably should deliver).

Four misconceptions are worth clearing up directly.

An audit is not primarily a fraud investigation. The auditor must consider fraud risk and must design procedures responsive to it (ISA 240). But an audit is designed to detect material misstatement, from whatever cause. Well-concealed fraud — especially fraud involving collusion between several people, or override of controls by senior management — can survive a properly conducted audit. This is an acknowledged limitation of the method, not a secret.

An audit does not value the business. A balance sheet is not a price tag. Most assets sit at historical cost less depreciation, not at what anyone would pay for them. A company with 10 billion soum of net assets might be worth 40 billion or might be worth nothing.

An audit does not confirm the business will survive. The auditor assesses whether the going concern assumption is appropriate, generally over at least twelve months from the reporting date (ISA 570). Companies have failed within a year of a clean opinion without the auditor having done anything wrong. The audit reports on a period that has already ended; the future is not the subject.

An audit does not mean the business is well run. A badly managed, unprofitable, poorly governed company can receive a perfectly clean opinion — provided its financial statements accurately report that it is badly managed, unprofitable and poorly governed. The opinion addresses the honesty of the account, not the quality of what is being accounted for.

The word "audit" means two different things in Uzbekistan

This is the section most likely to save a reader real trouble, and it is specific to our jurisdiction.

In Uzbekistan, "audit" is a legal term in two unrelated bodies of law, with two different meanings.

Article 137 of the Tax Code sets out the types of tax inspection, and one of them is called, in the official terminology, налоговый аудит — "tax audit." It is a form of state inspection: a comprehensive examination of whether taxes have been calculated and paid correctly over a given period, conducted by the tax authority, generally on taxpayers selected through risk analysis. Alongside it sit the desk inspection (камеральная проверка) and the field inspection (выездная проверка). Transfer pricing has its own regime of tax control, set out separately in Section VI of the Code (from Article 176).

That is one meaning.

Separately, the Law "On Auditing Activity" (ЗРУ-677 of 25 February 2021, in force from 27 May 2021) governs audit as a licensed professional service: an independent examination of financial statements by a registered audit organisation, resulting in an audit report.

These two things share a word and share almost nothing else. Confusing them is common, and it leads to real errors — companies who think a clean tax inspection means their accounts are audited, and companies who think an audit report protects them from the tax authority. Neither is true.

The practical summary: a tax inspection asks whether you paid the state enough. An audit asks whether what you told everyone else was true. A company can pass one and fail the other, in either direction.

The wider family of engagements

While we are drawing distinctions, several other things are routinely called "audits" and are not:

  • Internal audit is performed by employees of the company (or an outsourced function) and reports to the board or the audit committee. Its purpose is to improve internal control and risk management. It is not independent of the company and produces no public opinion.
  • Due diligence is an investigation commissioned by a buyer, investor or lender, scoped to their specific concerns. It has no standard form and expresses no opinion on the financial statements as a whole.
  • A review engagement (ISRE 2400) is a lower level of assurance — the practitioner states that nothing came to their attention suggesting the statements are materially misstated. Less work, weaker conclusion, lower fee. Useful, but not an audit.
  • Agreed-upon procedures (ISRS 4400) means the practitioner performs exactly the steps the client specified and reports the findings. No opinion, no assurance. The client draws their own conclusions.

If someone offers you a cheap "audit," establish which of these you are actually buying.

When is an audit required, and when is it a choice?

Mandatory audit

Article 35 of the Law on Auditing Activity sets out the categories of entity whose annual financial statements are subject to mandatory audit. The list is built around three ideas, and if you understand the ideas you can usually predict the list:

  1. Entities holding other people's money. Banks, insurers, investment funds, exchanges. Depositors and policyholders have no practical way to monitor these institutions themselves, and the consequences of failure fall on thousands of people who had no part in the decisions.
  2. Entities with dispersed or public ownership. Joint-stock companies, issuers of securities. The classic agency problem in its purest form: many owners, none of whom can walk into the office and look at the ledger.
  3. Entities large enough that their failure affects others. Companies above specified size thresholds, entities with state participation. Their suppliers, employees, lenders and tax authorities carry a risk they did not choose.

In full, Article 35 makes the annual audit mandatory for:

  • All joint-stock companies
  • Banks and other credit institutions
  • Insurance organisations
  • Exchanges
  • Investment and other funds that pool money from legal entities and individuals
  • Agricultural cooperatives
  • State unitary enterprises, and legal entities with a state share in their charter capital at the levels set by law
  • Funds formed from contributions paid by legal entities and individuals

Any other commercial organisation must also be audited if, for the year, it meets any two of these three criteria:

  • Total assets on the balance sheet above 100,000 BRV (base calculation values)
  • Revenue from sales of goods, works and services above 200,000 BRV
  • Average headcount above 100 employees

At the base calculation value in effect from 1 September 2026 (440,000 soum), that means assets above 44 billion soum and revenue above 88 billion soum. The BRV is revised regularly, so check the current value before relying on these figures.

Entities subject to mandatory audit must file a copy of the audit report with the tax authorities within 15 days of the audit being completed, and in any event no later than 15 June of the year following the reporting year.

IFRS and the expanding perimeter

Two instruments have steadily widened the population of Uzbek entities reporting under IFRS, and therefore the population needing an audit against IFRS rather than national standards.

Presidential Resolution PQ-4611 (ПП-4611) of 24 February 2020 required joint-stock companies, commercial banks, insurance organisations and large taxpayers to organise their accounting under IFRS from 1 January 2021 and to prepare IFRS financial statements from the end of 2021. Entities that adopt IFRS voluntarily are exempted from filing statements under national accounting standards.

Presidential Resolution PQ-282 of 15 September 2025 introduced the legal status of Public Interest Entity (PIE) and a public register maintained by the Ministry of Economy and Finance. According to the Ministry, the register formed in 2026 contained 449 organisations: listed companies, banks and other financial institutions, investment funds and exchanges, large state-participation enterprises, and large private companies meeting asset, revenue and headcount criteria. An entity on the register must organise its accounting on the basis of IFRS from 1 January of the following year — for those included in 2026, from 1 January 2027 — and, "following the results of the next (second) year", prepare IFRS financial statements and publish them together with an independent audit report. The register is updated annually. What this means in practice: a transition checklist for PIEs.

The direction of travel is not ambiguous. The threshold for "you must be audited to international standards" has been falling for six years and continues to fall.

Voluntary audit

Everyone else may choose. In practice, companies choose an audit for one of four reasons, and it is worth being clear about which one applies before commissioning one:

To raise money. A bank's credit committee, an equity investor, or a development finance institution will discount unaudited figures heavily or refuse to consider them. The audit is not a formality here; it changes the price of capital.

To sell, or to bring in a partner. Audited statements for the preceding two or three years shorten due diligence, narrow the range of warranties a buyer demands, and remove the buyer's most effective negotiating argument — that your numbers are unverified.

To solve a problem between owners. Where some shareholders manage the business and others do not, an independent audit converts an argument about trust into a question of fact. This is the agency problem at the kitchen-table level, and the audit is the cheapest available answer.

To find out. Some founders simply want to know whether what their accountant tells them is correct — before a bank, a buyer or the tax authority tells them it isn't.

Who actually reads the report

An audit report has no value in itself. It has value because specific people change specific decisions after reading it.

Shareholders who do not manage. The original audience, and still the conceptual core. The audit is how an owner who is not in the building forms a justified belief about what happened to their money.

Banks and lenders. A credit committee uses audited statements to size a facility, set an interest rate, and define covenants. Audited figures also matter after the loan: under IFRS 9, banks estimate expected credit losses on their loan book, and the quality of the borrower's financial information feeds directly into that estimate. A borrower with unaudited accounts is, from the bank's perspective, a more expensive asset to hold. That cost is passed back in the form of a higher rate, more security, or no loan.

Investors and acquirers. In a transaction, the gap between audited and unaudited statements is paid for by the seller — through a lower price, a larger escrow, or wider warranties and indemnities.

The tax authority. Not as a substitute for its own inspection powers, but as a risk signal. Financial statements accompanied by a clean independent opinion and consistent with the tax returns filed look different, in a risk-analysis system, from statements that are neither.

Counterparties. A supplier deciding whether to extend 90-day credit, or a customer deciding whether to pay a large deposit in advance, is making a credit decision with no better information than a lender has.

Employees, and the public. For a large employer, the financial statements are the only public account of whether the entity can meet its obligations. For a bank or an insurer, the "public" includes everyone with a deposit or a policy.

Notice the common feature: in every case the reader is someone who cannot look at the books themselves. The audit exists to serve people who are absent. If everyone who needed the information could simply walk in and check, there would be no audit profession.

Why audit is such an enormous institution in the United States

Readers often sense that audit matters more in America than it does here, and they are right. The reason is structural, and understanding it tells you where Uzbekistan is heading.

In the United States, ordinary households own companies. Not directly, for the most part, but through pension funds, 401(k) retirement plans, mutual funds and index funds. A schoolteacher in Ohio is a part-owner of several thousand businesses she will never visit, run by people she will never meet, in industries she knows nothing about.

That is the agency problem at national scale. The only thing standing between that teacher and a complete inability to know anything is a chain of reported financial information — and an auditor's signature at the end of it.

The institutional history is a sequence of disasters followed by legislation.

1929 and after. The stock market crash and the Depression exposed how little reliable information investors had been given. Congress responded with the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which created the Securities and Exchange Commission and required companies selling securities to the public to file financial statements audited by an independent accountant. The modern audit profession in America is substantially a creation of those two statutes.

2001–2002. Enron, a company that had been among the largest in the United States, collapsed after it emerged that its reported profits depended on structures designed to keep debt off its balance sheet. Its auditor, Arthur Andersen — one of the five largest firms in the world, with tens of thousands of employees — was destroyed along with it. WorldCom followed. The damage was not only to the shareholders of those companies; it was to the credibility of every audited financial statement in the market.

The legislative response was the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, which remains the most significant intervention in the profession's history. It created the PCAOB to inspect audit firms rather than leaving the profession to police itself. It required chief executives and chief financial officers to personally certify the financial statements. Its famous Section 404 required management to assess, and the auditor to report on, the effectiveness of internal control over financial reporting. It banned audit firms from providing many consulting services to the companies they audit — recognising that an auditor earning large fees from a client for other work is not independent of that client in any meaningful sense.

The lesson Americans drew, at enormous cost, is the one worth importing:

The value of an audit lies entirely in the independence of the auditor. The moment the auditor has an interest in the answer, the signature stops carrying information — and because nobody can tell from the outside which signatures still carry information, confidence in all of them falls at once.

Why this is becoming relevant here. Uzbekistan's capital market is young but no longer theoretical. Shares trade on the Tashkent exchange. State enterprises are being privatised. Corporate bonds are being issued. International lenders and development finance institutions operate here at scale. Foreign direct investment keeps arriving. Each of these creates a new group of people who hold a financial interest in a business they cannot inspect — which is to say, each creates new demand for credible financial information.

The PIE register, mandatory IFRS, published statements with audit reports attached: these are the institutional architecture that becomes necessary once ownership and control separate at scale. The United States built it between 1933 and 2002, mostly in response to catastrophes. Uzbekistan is building it now, with the advantage of knowing how the story goes.

Reading an audit report

If you are ever handed one, here is how to read it.

Begin at the end of the first section, where the opinion is stated. There are four possibilities, and they are not shades of the same colour — they are categorically different statements.

Unmodified ("clean") opinion. The statements present fairly, in all material respects, in accordance with the stated framework. This is the normal outcome and the one every company wants.

Qualified opinion. "Except for" a specified matter, the statements present fairly. Something is wrong, or something could not be verified, and it is material — but it is contained, and the rest of the statements can be relied upon. Read the basis paragraph carefully; it tells you exactly what the problem is.

Adverse opinion. The statements do not present fairly. The problems are material and pervasive. This is a serious statement and it is rare.

Disclaimer of opinion. The auditor was unable to obtain sufficient evidence to form any opinion at all, and the possible effects are material and pervasive. In practice this usually means records were missing or access was obstructed. For most readers, a disclaimer should be treated as worse news than a qualification, because it means nobody knows.

Then read what surrounds the opinion.

An Emphasis of Matter paragraph draws attention to something properly disclosed in the statements that the auditor considers fundamental to understanding them. It does not modify the opinion — but it is there because the auditor wanted to be sure you did not miss it.

A material uncertainty related to going concern means the auditor has concluded that events or conditions exist that may cast significant doubt on the entity's ability to continue operating. If you read nothing else, read this.

Key Audit Matters (ISA 701, reported for listed entities and where otherwise required) set out the matters that required the most auditor attention. They are the closest thing to a map of where the judgement and the risk actually sat.

And the single most important interpretive point:

A clean opinion is a statement about the financial statements, not about the business. It means the account is faithful. It does not mean the account is good news.

The limits, stated honestly

A profession that oversells itself invites the expectation gap it then complains about. So, plainly:

Sampling means some transactions are never examined. This is inherent and unavoidable. The response is risk-based selection, not exhaustive testing.

Materiality means small errors are tolerated. A misstatement below the materiality threshold may be known to the auditor and left uncorrected, because it would not change any reasonable reader's decision.

Collusion defeats controls. Audit procedures rely substantially on internal controls and on documentary evidence. Where several people cooperate to produce false documents, detection becomes considerably harder.

Management override is the hardest risk in the profession. Senior management can, by definition, instruct people to depart from normal procedure. ISA 240 treats this as a presumed risk in every audit for exactly that reason.

Accounting requires judgement, and judgement has range. Whether a receivable is recoverable, whether an asset is impaired, how long equipment will last — these are estimates. The auditor assesses whether management's estimate is reasonable and properly disclosed, not whether it is the single correct number, because there isn't one.

And the structural threat: the audited company pays the auditor. This is an acknowledged weakness in the design of the institution worldwide, and it is why independence requirements, rotation rules, prohibitions on certain non-audit services, licensing and external quality inspection exist. They are not bureaucracy. They are the mechanism that keeps the signature meaningful, and every major audit failure in history has involved their erosion.

Do you need one?

A short decision guide.

You are legally required to have one if your entity falls within Article 35 of the Law on Auditing Activity, or within the IFRS and PIE regimes described above. Check this first; the answer is not a matter of preference.

You should strongly consider one if any of the following is true:

  • You intend to raise bank financing or equity investment within the next 12–24 months
  • You have shareholders who are not involved in management
  • You are preparing to sell the business or admit a new partner
  • You are a subsidiary, joint venture or major supplier of an entity that is itself subject to mandatory IFRS audit — their obligations will reach you
  • Your business is approaching the size thresholds in the legislation
  • You do not currently have independent confirmation that your accounts are correct, and the consequences of them being wrong are large

You probably do not need one yet if you are small, owner-managed, not borrowing, not raising capital, and not approaching any statutory threshold. What you need at that stage is accurate bookkeeping and correct tax filings. An audit of unreliable records is an expensive way to be told your records are unreliable — fix the records first.

The one-paragraph version

A business is run by people who know what is happening inside it, and it is financed, supplied, banked, taxed and in some cases owned by people who do not. That gap is not a failure of anyone's character; it is the structure of modern commerce. Left alone, it makes capital expensive, deals slow, and trust impossible to establish. An audit closes the gap by inserting someone who works for neither side, applies a defined professional standard, and signs their name to a conclusion they can be held to. The signature is not a formality and it is not a guarantee. It is the mechanism by which a company's own account of itself becomes something a stranger can rely on.
